# Reducing waiting times at the Riverbend Outpatient Clinic: a discrete-event simulation study

A. Fictional and B. Imaginary — Department of Operational Research, University of Nowhere (fictional)

## 1. Purpose of the model

The Riverbend Outpatient Clinic (a fictional 4-doctor, 3-nurse clinic) has seen its mean waiting time to consultation rise from 18 to 31 minutes over two years. Management asked whether adding one nurse, pooling the doctors across specialties, or both, would bring waiting times back under 20 minutes without lowering staff utilisation below 60 %. We built a discrete-event simulation (DES) model of the clinic's patient flow to answer that question. The model is intended for capacity planning at this clinic only; it is not intended as a generic clinic model.

## 2. Model outputs

Three quantitative performance measures are reported for every scenario:

- Mean waiting time to consultation (minutes), from arrival at check-in to the start of the consultation, averaged over all patients who complete a consultation during the data-collection period.
- 95th percentile of waiting time to consultation (minutes), computed over the same patients.
- Utilisation of nurses and of doctors (%), computed as busy time divided by scheduled time over the data-collection period.

All measures are calculated after the warm-up period and are averaged across replications; 95 % confidence intervals are computed with the t-distribution across the replication means.

## 3. Experimentation aims

The model was used to compare four staffing scenarios:

| Scenario | Description | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| S0 | Base case: 3 nurses, 4 doctors each seeing only their own specialty | Current operation |
| S1 | S0 plus one additional nurse | Cheapest intervention proposed by management |
| S2 | Doctors pooled: any doctor sees any patient | Zero-cost intervention proposed by clinicians |
| S3 | S1 and S2 combined | Upper bound of what is affordable |

We did not use a designed experiment or simulation optimisation; the four scenarios were chosen by management and the research team.

## 4. Model logic

### 4.1 Overview

Figure 1 is a process-flow diagram of the clinic: patients arrive, check in at reception, are triaged by a nurse, wait for a consultation with a doctor, and either leave or proceed to a blood test before leaving. A more detailed activity-cycle diagram is provided as Figure S1 in the supplementary material.

### 4.2 Base model logic

A patient arrives either with an appointment or as a walk-in. All patients check in at reception (two receptionists). Patients then wait for a nurse for triage, where an acuity level (1 = urgent to 3 = routine) is assigned. After triage, patients join the consultation queue for a doctor of their specialty (in the base case) and are served in order of acuity, then first-come-first-served within acuity. After the consultation, 28 % of patients are sent for a blood test (one phlebotomy room, one nurse) and then leave; the remaining 72 % leave directly. A doctor who finishes a consultation immediately takes the next patient in his or her queue if one is waiting.

### 4.3 Scenario logic

Scenarios differ only in parameter values (the number of nurses, and a flag that pools the doctors so that any doctor may see any patient); the process logic described in 4.2 is unchanged in all scenarios.

### 4.4 Algorithms

Appointment slots are generated by the following scheduling algorithm, which mimics the clinic's manual booking rule:

```
for each clinic day d:
    for each doctor j:
        slots[j] = 15-minute slots from 09:00 to 12:45 and 14:00 to 16:45
        mark the first slot after each hour as a 'catch-up' slot (no booking)
    for each appointment request r in order of request time:
        assign r to the earliest free non-catch-up slot of the requested specialty
        if no slot within 14 days: assign r to the earliest free slot of any specialty
```

### 4.5 Components

**Entities.** Patients. Attributes: arrival time; appointment flag (appointment / walk-in); specialty (A or B); acuity (1–3); blood-test flag; the time stamps of each activity start and end.

**Activities.** Check-in (reception), triage (nurse), consultation (doctor), blood test (phlebotomy nurse). Routing: check-in → triage → consultation → (blood test with probability 0.28) → exit.

**Resources.** 2 receptionists (used by check-in), 3 nurses (used by triage and, for one nurse at a time, by the blood test), 4 doctors (used by consultation; 2 of specialty A, 2 of specialty B).

**Queues.** All queues are first-in-first-out except the consultation queue, which is prioritised by acuity (1 before 2 before 3) and first-in-first-out within acuity. No reneging, balking or jockeying occurs in the model (patients who arrive always wait for service). Queues have unlimited capacity; the waiting room's 24 seats were not modelled as a constraint.

**Entry/exit points.** Patients enter at reception. Walk-in arrivals follow a non-homogeneous Poisson process with an hourly rate profile (Table 2), sampled by thinning with a maximum rate of 9 per hour. Appointment arrivals occur at the scheduled time plus a lateness offset (Table 2). Patients exit after the consultation or after the blood test.

## 5. Data

### 5.1 Data sources

- Electronic health record extract from the clinic for 1 January to 30 June 2024 (n = 4,812 visits): arrival, check-in, triage, consultation and blood-test time stamps.
- Time-and-motion observation of consultations by the research team in May 2024 (n = 120 consultations).
- Interviews with six staff members (2 doctors, 3 nurses, 1 receptionist) in May 2024 to establish routing rules and the booking rule.

### 5.2 Pre-processing

Thirty-seven visits with negative or zero activity durations (time-stamp errors) were removed before fitting distributions. No interpolation was needed. Walk-in arrival counts were aggregated by hour of day across the 125 clinic days in the extract.

### 5.3 Input parameters

Table 2 lists all input parameters with the values used in the base case and, where different, in the scenarios.

| Parameter | Distribution / value | Source (sample size) |
|---|---|---|
| Walk-in arrival rate by hour (patients/hour) | 09:00 4.1; 10:00 7.8; 11:00 8.9; 12:00 5.2; 14:00 6.3; 15:00 6.0; 16:00 3.4 | EHR extract (n = 2,140 walk-ins) |
| Appointment lateness (minutes) | Normal(mean 3.2, sd 8.5), truncated at −20 and +40 | EHR extract (n = 2,672) |
| Check-in duration (minutes) | Triangular(min 1, mode 2, max 5) | Staff interviews (n = 6) |
| Triage duration (minutes) | Gamma(shape 4.5, scale 1.6) | EHR extract (n = 4,775) |
| Consultation duration (minutes) | Lognormal(mu 2.62, sigma 0.41) | Time-and-motion sample (n = 120) |
| Blood-test duration (minutes) | Triangular(min 3, mode 6, max 12) | Staff interviews (n = 6) |
| Probability of blood test | 0.28 | EHR extract (n = 4,775) |
| Acuity mix (1 / 2 / 3) | 0.12 / 0.45 / 0.43 | EHR extract (n = 4,775) |
| Number of nurses | 3 (S0, S2); 4 (S1, S3) | Scenario definition |
| Doctor pooling | off (S0, S1); on (S2, S3) | Scenario definition |

Theoretical distributions were chosen by comparing candidate families (exponential, gamma, lognormal, normal) with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic and the Akaike information criterion on the extract; the family with the lowest AIC was retained in every case. No time-dependent parameters other than the hourly arrival profile, and no correlation between inputs, are modelled.

### 5.4 Assumptions

Where data were unavailable we assumed: (i) check-in and blood-test durations follow the triangular distributions elicited from staff, because the EHR does not record them; (ii) staff take no breaks during clinic hours; (iii) the waiting room never fills; (iv) patients never leave before being seen. Assumptions (ii)–(iv) were confirmed as reasonable by the staff interviewed.

## 6. Experimentation

### 6.1 Initialisation

The clinic is modelled as a non-terminating system (the booking backlog carries over from day to day). A warm-up period of 4 weeks was used. The model starts empty: no patients are pre-loaded into any queue or activity.

### 6.2 Run length

Each replication runs for 30 simulated weeks: 4 weeks of warm-up followed by 26 weeks of data collection. The simulation time unit is minutes.

### 6.3 Estimation approach

Fifty independent replications were run for each scenario. The number of replications was chosen so that the half-width of the 95 % confidence interval on mean waiting time was below 5 % of the mean in the base case (reached at 38 replications; 50 was used for margin). Batch means were not used.

## 7. Implementation

### 7.1 Software

The model was written in Python 3.11.4 using SimPy 4.0.1, with pandas 2.1.0 and NumPy 1.26.0 for data handling, on Windows 11 Pro version 23H2 (build 22631).

### 7.2 Model execution

SimPy implements the process-interaction world view: each patient is a process that requests resources in sequence. When patients compete for a doctor, the consultation queue is served by acuity then arrival time, as stated in 4.5; nurses are allocated first-come-first-served. The model is run on a single core; it is not parallel or distributed.

### 7.3 System specification

Each replication takes about 40 seconds on a laptop with an Intel Core i7-1260P processor and 16 GB of RAM; a full scenario of 50 replications takes about 33 minutes.

## 8. Results (summary)

In the base case the mean waiting time was 31.4 minutes (95 % CI 30.2–32.6). Scenario S2 (pooling) reduced it to 19.1 minutes (18.3–19.9) with doctor utilisation of 71 %; S1 alone reduced it only to 28.0 minutes; S3 reached 16.5 minutes but lowered nurse utilisation to 55 %.

## 9. Conclusion

Pooling the doctors meets the management target at no cost; adding a nurse is not justified on waiting-time grounds.
