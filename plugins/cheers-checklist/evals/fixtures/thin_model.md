# Nurse-led telephone follow-up after hip fracture surgery: costs and quality-adjusted life years

J. Unreal and K. Notaperson — Northshire Orthopaedic Services Research Group (fictional)

## Background

After surgical repair of a hip fracture, older patients in Northshire (fictional) are followed up at the orthopaedic outpatient clinic at 6 and 12 weeks. Many patients find the trip difficult, and early problems such as falls at home are often detected late. The regional health board is considering replacing the clinic visits with nurse-led telephone follow-up and needs to know whether doing so would offer value for money. We therefore estimated the costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) of nurse-led telephone follow-up compared with usual clinic follow-up.

## Methods

We followed a hypothetical cohort of 1,000 patients aged 80 years (75 % women; 40 % living alone; 30 % with cognitive impairment) discharged home after surgical repair of a hip fracture. Northshire has a tax-funded health service; patients discharged home receive community physiotherapy, and residential care is publicly funded after a means test, so admissions to residential care are a major cost driver in this region.

Patients receive either nurse-led telephone follow-up (calls at 2, 6 and 12 weeks by an orthopaedic nurse, with a home visit if problems are detected) or usual follow-up at the orthopaedic outpatient clinic at 6 and 12 weeks.

The cohort moves between four health states — at home, readmitted to hospital, in residential care, and dead — in yearly cycles over a lifetime horizon, with transition probabilities that differ between the two follow-up strategies for the first year only (Table 1). Each health state has a utility weight. The measure of benefit was QALYs; falls and hospital readmissions were counted as harms.

Costs of telephone calls, home visits, clinic visits, hospital readmissions and residential care were valued with the regional unit cost tariff. Costs and QALYs were discounted at 3.5 % per year.

## Results

Mean lifetime costs per patient were N$ 41,300 with telephone follow-up and N$ 43,900 with clinic follow-up, and mean QALYs were 2.94 and 2.89. Telephone follow-up was therefore less costly and more effective (dominant). The results were most sensitive to the readmission probability in year 1: telephone follow-up remained dominant unless its effect on readmission was less than one third of the base-case effect.

## Discussion

Nurse-led telephone follow-up after hip fracture surgery appears to save money and improve quality-adjusted survival compared with clinic follow-up. The estimates depend on the effect on readmissions in the first year, which comes from a small before-and-after audit, and the results may not apply to patients discharged to residential care. Replacing clinic visits with telephone follow-up could free clinic capacity for patients who need examination.
